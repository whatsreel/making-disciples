# Argument Index: Discipleship
### Organized by reasoning, not by author

Each entry: the claim, the case for it, the strongest counter, the rejoinder, and the author's rating. Authors appear only as shorthand for a line of argument. The rating is the author's assessment, not a verdict — argue with it.

Sections run in the order the session document uses: the interpretive rules first and by themselves, then those rules applied to Jesus' ministry, then what discipleship is, then who does it, then models, then the evidence claims people bring to the table. Positions grounded in Scripture come first throughout; historical and confessional material appears only where the claim at issue is itself a historical claim.

Ratings are the author's own: **firm** (one reading clearly stronger) · **contested** (live dispute, honest people differ) · **open** (no evidence either way). Each entry also carries the tags used in the interactive map: which column it sits in, whether it holds without leaning on Jesus’ example, and which of the two questions it bears on — *what* discipleship is, and *who* does it. Many positions bear on only one, or neither.

## A. Hermeneutics: how we answer, before we answer

This section is deliberately silent on the substance of the debate. It states the rules we will use. Sections B onward apply them.

### A1. "The interpretive rules have to be stated before they are applied."

**The rules in play.** Five are doing the work in this discussion.

1. *Grammatical-historical*: the meaning is what the author intended his first readers to understand — not what a word later came to mean in church usage.
2. *Usus loquendi*: a term's meaning is set by established usage — primarily the usage of the author in question, and secondarily that of the wider linguistic community he wrote in — not by etymology or by our theological vocabulary. Where an author has used a word repeatedly, that usage governs when he uses it again. The author's own usage carries first; other writers of the same period confirm that the sense was shared rather than idiosyncratic. **Note where this binds us:** an argument about Matthew's meaning has to be able to stand on Matthew before it appeals to Mark, Luke or John. C1 is built to meet that test.
3. *Context determines meaning*, at three levels: immediate passage, whole book, whole canon.
4. *Scripture interprets Scripture* (WCF 1.9): the clearer interprets the less clear, and the didactic material of the letters helps us read narrative rightly.
5. *Good and necessary consequence* (WCF 1.6): the whole counsel of God is either expressly set down in Scripture *or may be deduced from it* by good and necessary consequence. This is the rule that lets a duty be drawn from what Scripture records and not only from what it commands — and it is the rule the other four hand their results to. A conclusion drawn from Christ's conduct, or from what the apostles did, binds *if and only if* the deduction is good (the inference is sound) and necessary (it follows, rather than merely fits). It is the mechanism the whole discussion runs on: without it, narrative could never oblige anyone; with it, narrative obliges exactly as far as sound inference carries and no further. Both views need it, and both should be held to its two tests.

**Why this matters.** Nearly every disagreement ahead is really a disagreement about rule 2 or rule 3 — whether an author's established usage of a term governs a later command, and whether the letters restrict or extend what the Gospels show. Arguing conclusions without agreeing on rules produces two men quoting verses past each other.

**Author's rating: firm, and prior to everything else.**

*Method only — decides what counts as an answer, not what the answer is · Use: Lead*

### A2. "What do we do with narrative passages?"

**Case for caution.** Narrative reports what happened; it does not by itself commend it. Scripture records Abraham's lying, David's adultery, and the chaos of Judges without approval. Reading practice off narrative has produced most of the church's worst innovations.

**Case for use.** Narrative also teaches, and Scripture says so: Old Testament events "took place as examples for us" and "were written down for our instruction" (1 Cor 10:6, 11); "whatever was written in former days was written for our instruction" (Rom 15:4). The New Testament argues doctrine from narrative — Paul from Abraham (Gal 3), Hebrews from Melchizedek and the wilderness generation, James from Elijah (5:17).

**The working rule.** A narrative element carries normative weight when at least one holds: (a) Scripture states its meaning or commends it — "I have given you an example" (John 13:15); (b) it is commanded elsewhere in didactic material; (c) it is repeated as a deliberate pattern, especially by non-apostles after Pentecost; (d) it rests on unchanging theology rather than on a stage in redemptive history. It does *not* carry weight merely because someone in Scripture did it once.

**Corollary — precept governs pattern.** Where a precept exists, narrative illustrates it and the precept binds. Where no precept exists, pattern illustrates but does not bind. Christ's example is a case where a precept is already given: Scripture commands conformity to him (A5; 1 Pet 2:21; 1 John 2:6). So for his example the question is never whether it carries normative force, only which aspects carry it and by what warrant (A5), and that is what the working rule above sorts.

**Author's rating: firm as a method, and both views must be held to it.** It rules out "Jesus did it, therefore we must." It equally rules out "that's just narrative," which would delete church planting and plural eldership from Acts 14:23.

*Method only — decides what counts as an answer, not what the answer is · Use: Lead*

### A3. "Applying that rule yields three categories, not two."

**The framework.** Anything we observe in Scripture falls into one of three categories with respect to *us*:

1. **Binding on every church** — commanded, or interpreted by Scripture as pattern, or repeated after Pentecost by people without apostolic authority.
2. **Not binding on any church** — carrying unique redemptive-historical meaning, explicitly revised by Scripture, or discontinued by the apostles without censure.
3. **Wise but not binding** — neither commanded nor revised, but commended by example. Free to adopt; not free to impose on another's conscience (Rom 14:4–5).

**Why three and not two.** A two-category scheme forces everything not commanded into "irrelevant," which is false; and it invites the opposite error of promoting anything commendable into "binding." The third category is where most practical wisdom lives, and naming it is what keeps wisdom from hardening into law.

**Counter.** The categories are ours, not Scripture's, and people will sort self-servingly — putting whatever they already want into "required."

**Rejoinder.** The discipline is to sort before deciding. The test of honesty is whether you can name things you personally favor that land in category 3 and therefore cannot be imposed on anyone. If nothing you favor lands there, you are not using the tool.

**Author's rating: firm as a method.** Treating the merely wise as binding is the Galatian error (Gal 3:3; 5:1). Treating the binding as optional is hearing without doing (Jas 1:22).

*Method only — decides what counts as an answer, not what the answer is · Use: Lead*

## B. Applying the rules to Jesus' ministry

### A4. "Christ himself extends what the Twelve received to all later believers."

**Case.** The standing objection to every Gospel argument in this paper is that the Twelve were apostles and nothing normative transfers. In the intercessory prayer Christ answers it in his own voice. Having prayed for the Eleven, he says: "**Neither pray I for these alone, but for them also which shall believe on me through their word**" (John 17:20). Bruce, summarizing, notes that Jesus "intimates that He desires for the parties next to be prayed for the same things He has already asked for his disciples: preservation in the truth, and from the evil in the world, and sanctification by the truth." The sending language sits in the same prayer — "as thou hast sent me into the world, even so have I also sent them into the world" (17:18).

This belongs among the method questions rather than the positions, because it is a **warrant for transfer** rather than one more instance of it. The transfer from the Twelve to the later church is not our inference. He makes it.

**Counter.** What is extended in John 17 is preservation, protection and sanctification — not training methods, not structures, not practices. It licenses transfer in general and settles no particular case.

**Rejoinder.** Agreed, and the limit should be stated before the argument is used. A4 answers whether *anything* passes from the Twelve to us. It does not tell us *what*, and it cannot be allowed to do A2's sorting work. Each bin still has to be argued on its own evidence. What A4 removes is only the blanket objection — the move that says the Gospels are apostolic territory and we may take nothing from them at all.

**Author's rating: firm as a warrant; silent on particulars.**

*Method only — decides what counts as an answer, not what the answer is · Needs Jesus' example to bind · Use: Lead*

### A5. "Christ's example is not narrative awaiting sorting. Scripture commands conformity to it."

**Why this has to come first.** A2 and A3 ask what we do with narrative passages and how to sort them. Useful as far as it goes — but placed first, it concedes the frame. It treats Christ as one more figure in a story whose conduct we may or may not find normative, and it leaves the door open to the blanket refusal: *nothing from Jesus' example counts*. That refusal is not available to a Christian, and the method section should say so before the sorting begins.

**Case.** Christ is the one person the New Testament explicitly commands us to copy, and it does so in precept, in the letters — not in narrative.

- 1 Peter 2:21: Christ suffered for us, "leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps." The noun is *hypogrammos*, used nowhere else in the New Testament. It is the schoolroom word for a writing-copy set beneath a child's hand so the letters can be traced. Peter did not reach for a word meaning *inspiration*. He reached for one meaning *reproduce this*.
- 1 John 2:6: "He that saith he abideth in him ought himself also so to walk, even as he walked." The test of abiding is conformity of walk.
- Philippians 2:5: "Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus."
- Matthew 11:29: "learn of me" — Christ makes himself, not merely his doctrine, the object of the learning.
- 1 Corinthians 11:1: "Be ye followers of me, even as I also am of Christ." Paul's imitability is derivative; Christ's is the standard it borrows from. Note what this does to C12: the imitation of people rests on the prior imitation of Christ, and cannot be separated from it.

**And the confession supplies the mechanism.** WCF 1.6 states that the whole counsel of God is either expressly set down in Scripture or may be deduced from it by good and necessary consequence. A conclusion drawn from Christ's recorded conduct is therefore a legitimate route to a binding obligation — provided the deduction really is good and necessary. That proviso is the whole of the work, and it is what A2 and A3 exist to do.

**Counter, and it is the right one.** These texts command conformity to Christ's *character* — his meekness, his patience under injustice, his love — not to his *methods*. 1 Peter 2:21 is about suffering unjustly; read it in context and the subject is slaves under harsh masters, not ministry technique. Nobody disputes that we must be meek as he was meek. It does not follow that we must train people the way he trained the Twelve.

**Rejoinder.** Conceded, and the concession is important enough to state in the paper rather than bury. A5 does not establish that any particular practice of Jesus binds us. It establishes something narrower and prior: that the blanket move is closed. The question can never be *whether* Christ's example carries normative force, only *which* aspects carry it and by what warrant. An elder who wants to exclude the training of the Twelve must now argue that specific case on its merits — he cannot set the Gospels aside wholesale, because Scripture commands him to trace the letters.

**Author's rating: firm as a method; silent on every particular.** This is a door, not a room. Its value is that it forecloses one move.


### B1. "Jesus' practice with the Twelve is normative for us."

**Case.** Scripture treats his conduct as pattern where it says so: John 13:15; 1 Pet 2:21; 1 Cor 11:1. "A disciple when fully trained will be like his teacher" is stated generally, not apostolically (Luke 6:40) — and Jesus uses the same axiom elsewhere, outside that context, about persecution: "it is enough for the disciple to be like his teacher" (Matt 10:24–25; cf. John 15:20). Note that Luke 6:40 sits in a warning about blind guides, so its first force is that a disciple becomes like whoever teaches him; the principle holds either way, but cite Matt 10:24–25 alongside it.

**Counter.** The Twelve were apostles, a unique and unrepeatable office (Acts 1:21–22; Eph 2:20; Rev 21:14).

**Rejoinder.** The office is unrepeatable; the practice was not confined to it. Seventy-two others were sent the same way with the same instructions and the same debriefing (Luke 10:1–24), and after Pentecost non-apostles did the same (Acts 8:4; 18:26).

**Author's rating: contested as a blanket claim, resolved by B2.** Anyone answering "normative" or "not normative" flatly has skipped the sorting.

### B2. "Which parts of Jesus' practice bind us, and which do not?"

**Binding on us** — commanded, interpreted by Scripture as pattern, or repeated after Pentecost by non-apostles: teaching toward obedience (Matt 28:20; Luke 6:40); cost (Luke 9:23; Rom 12:1); sharing life (1 Thess 2:8; Acts 20:20); choosing after prayer (Luke 6:12–13; Acts 13:2–3); being with before being sent (Mark 3:14 — noting the verse names them apostles, so the weight here rests on its repetition in 1 Thess 2:8 and Acts 20:20); sending and receiving the report back (Luke 9:1–10; 10:17–20, repeated at Acts 13:1–3 and 14:26–27); the widening of the pattern outward (Acts 1:8; 8:4; 2 Tim 2:2).

**Not binding on us** — belonging to a unique moment in redemptive history, revised by Scripture, or not continued by the apostles: the number twelve (Matt 19:28); three years (Acts 17:2; 18:11; 20:31); leaving trades (Luke 22:35–36; Acts 18:3); celibacy (Matt 19:11–12; 1 Cor 9:5); miracles (2 Cor 12:12; 1 Tim 5:23).

**Wise but not binding** — commended by example; free to adopt, not free to impose (Rom 14:4–5): explaining more deeply to a few (Mark 4:34); and the *architecture* by which the binding things are delivered — cohort sizes, tiers, training order, curricula.

**Note on the widening.** The substance is binding; only the staging is wise. Do not overstate Luke 10 here: the seventy-two were sent "ahead of him into every town where he himself was about to go" (10:1) — a mission function, not a training tier — and no text says the Twelve trained them. What it establishes is that the practice was never confined to the apostolic band.

**Author's rating: contested at the margins, and that is fine.** The lists above are my sorting under the A3 framework. Argue with individual placements; the method survives the argument.

## C. What discipleship is

### C1. "'Make disciples' is a command whose key term Matthew has already defined."

**Case.** Matt 28:19 has one finite imperative — *mathēteusate*, "make disciples." Going, baptizing, and teaching are participles describing how. But the imperative contains a noun Matthew never defines at that point, because he has spent twenty-seven chapters defining it: *mathētēs* appears roughly seventy times before this verse. **That count alone carries the argument**, and it is deliberately stated from Matthew before any other writer is mentioned, because rule 2 requires it. Seventy uses by one author, followed by that author deploying the term in a command without definition, is as strong a *usus loquendi* case as the New Testament offers. The wider evidence then confirms the sense was shared rather than Matthean: the noun occurs about 260 times in the New Testament (261 in the MGNT), all in the Gospels and Acts — John 78, Matthew 72, Mark 46, Luke 37, Acts 28. Corroboration, not foundation. A command cannot be obeyed apart from the meaning of its terms. Stated carefully: the term denotes an attached learner-follower, and the surrounding narrative shows what that attachment looked like in practice. The narrative is not merely decorative background to the command; it is how we know what the command's key word means.

The verb *mathēteuō* itself appears only four times in the New Testament (Matt 13:52; 27:57; 28:19; Acts 14:21), and in every case describes a person shaped into a follower, never a name added to a roll.

**Counter.** Two objections. First, Matt 28:19 arguably supplies its own definition in the participles — baptizing and teaching are what disciple-making consists of, so we need not reach back into the narrative. Second, and more serious: *mathētēs* never appears in the epistles. It occurs only in the Gospels and Acts. If the word were doing the theological work claimed for it, the apostles would have used it when instructing churches, and they did not — they wrote about brothers, saints, and imitators.

**Rejoinder.** On the first: participles of manner do not define the noun; they describe the action. And "teaching" in Matthew covers both public preaching (5:2) and private explanation (13:36), so the participles do not narrow it the way the objection needs. There is also a grammatical point the objection has to face. The verse has *three* participles, and *poreuthentes* — going — is regularly dropped when it is cited as though it read "baptizing and teaching." The grammar does distinguish them: *poreuthentes* is aorist, precedes an aorist main imperative in narrative, and is the standard case of attendant circumstance, which is why translations render it "go." *Baptizontes* and *didaskontes* are present participles following the verb and describe manner or means. But that distinction cuts against the objection rather than for it: attendant circumstance means going *shares the imperative's force*. It is not weaker than baptizing and teaching; it is commanded. And it appears on no list of the ordinary means of grace. So the participles cannot be read as confining disciple-making to the means as ordinarily listed. On the second: the absence in the letters is real, larger than a footnote, and should be conceded plainly. Twenty-one letters written to churches about formation never use the word. The best explanation on the other view is that post-Pentecost formation is conceived corporately and ecclesially — believers are brothers, saints, and members of a body, not pupils attached to an individual teacher — and that reading favors the means-centered position. It deserves an answer rather than a brush-off. The answer is that the substance persists under other words: "imitate me as I imitate Christ" (1 Cor 11:1), "you learned Christ" (Eph 4:20), "what you have learned and received and heard and seen in me, practice" (Phil 4:9), Paul as father to Timothy (1 Cor 4:15–17). Those are personal attachment language, in letters, to churches. But an honest reader should register that the vocabulary shifted and ask why.

**Author's rating: firm, and it is a stronger argument than the normativity claim in B1.** This does not require narrative to be prescriptive at all. The *command* is prescriptive; the narrative only tells us what its words mean. Anyone who resists reading practice out of the Gospels still has to accept this, because it is a lexical argument, not a narrative one.

### C1a–C1f. What the word carried: the six things a disciple received

C1 establishes that *mathētēs* had a settled meaning before Matt 28:19. These six entries say what that meaning contained. They are not six new positions; they are C1 itemized, and each can stand on its own. Taken together they are the answer to "what would you actually have us do?" — because each one names something a congregation either provides or does not.

Worth noting before the list: *cost* and *presence* are two of the six, and they are precisely the two elements TEAMS has no letter for. That is the honest test of the model, and it is a better use of these entries than treating them as a checklist.

### C1a. "A disciple is attached to a particular teacher, by name."

**Case.** The word never means a student of a subject. It means the follower of a person, and the attachment is always initiated and always specific. Matthew first, per rule 2: "Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men" (4:19) — a summons to a person, not to a program of study — and the disciples are those who come to *him* when he sits down to teach (5:1). Mark and John then confirm the same sense: he called to him whom he would, and appointed twelve that they should be with him (Mark 3:13–14); "ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you" (John 15:16), so the initiative is never the disciple's. This is the first thing the term carries into the Commission, and it is why an anonymous hearer, however regular, is not yet what the word describes.

**Counter.** That Christ calls does not mean we may select. The initiative in every one of these texts is his, not the church's.

**Rejoinder.** Conceded, and the boundary matters. This entry establishes that discipleship is personal and named, not that a session may choose whom to invest in. Whether concentration is legitimate for us is a separate argument and it lives at E3. Do not slide from "Christ chose" to "therefore we choose."

**Author's rating: firm on the definition; silent on selection.**

### C1b. "He kept them with him before he sent them anywhere."

**Case.** Presence comes first in the appointment and it is not decoration. Mark 3:14 states the order: "that they should be with him, and that he might send them forth." They watched him pray, eat, answer opponents, and handle interruption, and much of what they learned could not have been transmitted any other way. Paul describes his own ministry in the same terms, which is what lifts this off the narrative and onto firmer ground: "willing to have imparted... not the gospel of God only, but also our own souls" (1 Thess 2:8), and "taught you publickly, and from house to house" (Acts 20:20) — public ministry and personal ministry, named together and distinguished.

**Counter.** Paul planted churches he then wrote to from a distance, and the letters formed them. Presence is not strictly necessary for formation.

**Rejoinder.** The counter is right about necessity and wrong about ordinariness. The claim is not that presence is indispensable — overstated, it would imply the absent apostle failed his churches. The claim is that presence is the ordinary and assumed mode, which is why Acts 20:20 names house-to-house alongside public teaching rather than as a substitute for it.

**Author's rating: firm as the ordinary pattern; do not press it to indispensability.**

### C1c. "The teaching aimed at obedience, not at information transfer."

**Case.** What he taught them was how to do things — pray like this, forgive like this, serve like this — and he demonstrated before he explained (John 13:15). The Commission then names obedience as the content of the teaching rather than its hoped-for result: "teaching them to observe," where what is taught is keeping. The same method runs past the resurrection: "be ye followers of me" (1 Cor 11:1), and "what ye have both learned, and received, and heard, and seen in me, do" (Phil 4:9).

**Counter.** Preaching also aims at obedience, and the Spirit produces it. Nothing here requires a second structure.

**Rejoinder.** Granted that preaching aims at obedience; the question is whether anyone is positioned to notice whether it arrives. And note the guard: this describes sanctification, never the ground of anyone's standing. But the weight here is carried by C10, which makes the grammatical case at length. This entry is the narrative half of it. Where both are made, C10 carries the weight and this one supports it.

**Author's rating: firm, but derivative — C10 is the load-bearing version.**

### C1d. "He told them the cost before they were in."

**Case.** He was consistently harder on volunteers than on the reluctant. Count the cost, carry the cross daily, reckon whether you can finish the tower — and he said this to crowds who wanted to join, and several went away. Luke 9:23: "if any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily." Luke 14:25–33 is addressed to great multitudes, and it thinned them. Mark 8:34 issues the same call to the people as well as to the disciples. Any arrangement that recruits without naming the cost is doing something the word does not describe.

**Counter.** Cost is not a method of discipleship; it is a condition of it. Naming it tells a session nothing about what to build.

**Rejoinder.** True, and the concession cuts usefully — toward humility about any program. A curriculum that is pleasant to complete may not be forming anyone. Note also the guard that travels with this one: the cost is the fruit of grace, never its price. Discipleship with no call to die is cheap grace; discipleship where dying earns standing is Galatianism.

**Author's rating: firm on the definition; deliberately unhelpful about structure, which is itself the point.**

### C1e. "He corrected them, and restored them after failure."

**Case.** "Get thee behind me, Satan," said to the man he had just called blessed (Matt 16:23) — the sharpest rebuke in the Gospels, aimed at a disciple rather than at one of his critics. Then, after the denial, a charcoal fire and three questions answering three denials (John 21:15–19). Restoration is specific, not general. Correction and restoration are one motion in the Gospels, and the epistles command both to the church: "restore such an one in the spirit of meekness," addressed to the spiritual (Gal 6:1); "if any do err... and one convert him" (Jas 5:19–20), where the action belongs to any brother.

**Counter.** This is what church discipline is for, and we already have courts and a Book of Church Order to do it.

**Rejoinder.** That counter is correct and is conceded here, because it leads directly to D4. If the session wants to obey this without adopting any model, strengthening discipline and equipping parents is the near thing.

**Author's rating: firm, and it hands the argument to D4.**

### C1f. "He sent them out and took the report when they came back."

**Case.** Sending happened during the training, not after it, and it came with a debrief: they returned and told him all things (Matt 10:1; Mark 6:30). The seventy-two return with a report and he corrects what they are rejoicing in (Luke 10:17–20). Acts then shows the same shape in a church rather than around a person — Antioch sends, and Antioch receives the account (13:1–3; 14:26–27). **What that text does not show:** Antioch sent Barnabas and Saul, and Saul is an apostle on this paper's own account (Q2), so Acts 13 establishes the *shape* — a church commissions, a church takes the report — and not that ordinary members are sent this way. For that, the better texts are Acts 8:4, where the scattered believers and explicitly not the apostles preach as they go, and Acts 18:26, where two tentmakers instruct a preacher. The closing move is reproduction: "the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also" (2 Tim 2:2).

**Counter.** The sendings in the Gospels are apostolic commissions with authority over unclean spirits. Not every member is sent that way.

**Rejoinder.** The scope limit belongs up front. The transferable part is being sent and being accountable for it, not the authority attached to the Twelve — and not, on the Acts 13 evidence, the commissioning of apostles. What survives is a church that sends and expects an account, plus the plain fact that the people who carried the word out of Jerusalem were not officers (Acts 8:4). That is narrower than the entry first appears to claim, and it is what the entry should claim.

**Author's rating: firm on the pattern of sending-and-report; contested on scope, and weaker than it first looks once Acts 13 is read carefully.**

### C2. "Discipleship is the ordinary means of grace, delivered publicly."

**Case.** Scripture names the instruments: faith comes by hearing the preached word (Rom 10:14–17); the Supper is delivered and received (1 Cor 11:23–26); prayer is commanded first of all (1 Tim 2:1). The apostles gave themselves to prayer and the ministry of the word (Acts 6:4). God's word does its own work and does not return empty (Isa 55:10–11). Growth belongs to God through his appointed means (1 Cor 3:6–7).

**Counter.** Acts 2:42 — the verse most often cited as the apostolic pattern for this — lists four things, not three: the apostles' teaching, the fellowship, the breaking of bread, and the prayers. Four verses later the same passage has them in the temple *and* breaking bread in their homes (2:46). Paul summarizes his whole Ephesian ministry as teaching "in public and from house to house" (Acts 20:20). The public assembly is where the means are chiefly administered, not the whole of how they reach people.

**Rejoinder.** Fellowship and homes in Acts are the setting of the means, not additional means. The argument still forbids inventing instruments God has not given.

**Author's rating: firm against the strict form; the rejoinder is right about the limit.** Scripture does not confine the delivery of the word to the assembly. It also does not authorize instruments God has not appointed — which means a curriculum is a container for the word, never a means of grace.

### C3. "A disciple is defined by attachment and formation, not attendance."

**Case.** The word carries three years of meaning before the Great Commission uses it: called (Matt 4:19), appointed "to be with him" and then sent out (Mark 3:14), taught privately (Mark 4:34), costed in front of crowds (Luke 14:25–33), sent and debriefed (Luke 9:1–10), abiding and bearing fruit (John 15:4–8). Every one of those is a relationship word. None is an attendance word.

**Counter.** The word names a *status*, not a method. Knowing a disciple is attached and formed does not tell a session what to build. And Matt 28:19 supplies its own method — baptizing and teaching.

**Rejoinder.** Granted on method. But status is enough to settle the question actually in dispute: a person attached to no one does not match the word's usage in any Gospel. And "teach" in the Gospels covers both public preaching (Matt 5:2) and private explanation (Matt 13:36; Mark 4:34), so the Commission's own method-words are not pulpit-only.

**Author's rating: firm on status, contested on method.** The strongest single argument in the discussion — and it establishes less than advocates of any particular model want it to.

### C4. "Being sent and reproducing is part of the definition, not an add-on."

**Case.** The Twelve were appointed both "to be with him" and "to be sent out to preach" (Mark 3:14) — the two are one appointment. They were sent (Luke 9:1–6), returned, and reported (9:10). The pattern widened past them: seventy-two others were sent the same way and debriefed the same way (Luke 10:1–24), and the sending is extended to the whole church at the end of every Gospel (Matt 28:19; Luke 24:47; John 20:21). Paul then makes it explicit: entrust it to those who will teach others (2 Tim 2:2); older women train younger (Titus 2:3–5).

**Counter.** Careful with the Twelve-then-seventy-two step. Luke does not say the Twelve trained or sent the seventy-two — Jesus did. That text shows the practice *widening beyond the apostolic band*, which is real and useful, but it is not an instance of disciples reproducing themselves. The actual evidence for reproduction is in Acts and the letters.

**Rejoinder.** Accepted. State the seventy-two as evidence that the practice was never confined to the Twelve, and state reproduction from Acts 8:4, Acts 18:26, 2 Tim 2:2, and Titus 2:3–5.

**Author's rating: firm, once the argument is stated precisely.** A believer who is fed for decades and feeds no one is a failure of formation, not a private preference (Heb 5:12; Judg 2:10). But do not overclaim what Luke 10 says.

### C5. "Attendance at worship and Bible study is not discipleship."

**Case.** The argument rests on what the word means, not on proof texts against attendance — 1 Cor 8:1 and 2 Tim 3:7 are about idol food and false teachers' prey, and should not be borrowed here. It rests on this: attendance is the means by which disciples are fed (Heb 10:25), and the commands that constitute the disciple's life assume proximity. "Exhort one another every day" (Heb 3:13), "bear one another's burdens" (Gal 6:2), "confess your sins to one another" (Jas 5:16), "share our own selves" (1 Thess 2:8) cannot be done from a pew or a lecture seat.

**Counter.** This is a false dichotomy. Almost no one holds that attendance alone suffices; the means-centered position includes discipline, catechizing, family worship, and pastoral visitation. Arguing against "attendance only" answers a position few hold.

**Rejoinder.** Agreed as to theory. The question is practice — whether a church's assignment of responsibility, time, and elder attention reflects anything beyond attendance. That is an empirical question about a church's own practice, not a theological one about anyone's stated view.

**Author's rating: firm theologically; the counter is right that few hold "attendance only," so the argument bears on a church's practice, not on anyone's stated view.**

### C6. "Most of our members never signed up to be disciples — and we are saved by grace through faith, not by discipleship."

**The tension.** If a disciple is someone attached, formed, costed, and sent, and most Western Christians are none of those things, then either they are not Christians — which we will not say — or discipleship is optional, which the texts will not allow. Both horns are unacceptable, and the discussion stalls here unless we say why.

**What Scripture does not permit.** There is no category in the New Testament of a saved non-disciple. Acts simply calls church members "the disciples" — when the widows are neglected (6:1), when Saul tries to join in Jerusalem (9:26), when Paul and Barnabas strengthen the churches (14:22). It is the ordinary word for a member. The note that "the disciples were first called Christians in Antioch" (11:26) points the same way, though it is a remark about a nickname and should not carry the argument alone. "Disciple" is not a rank above "Christian."

**What Scripture equally does not permit.** Discipleship is not a condition of justification. We are saved by grace through faith, and not of works (Eph 2:8–9); God justifies the ungodly (Rom 4:5); a man is not justified by works of the law (Gal 2:16). Nothing in this discussion may be allowed to add a requirement to the gospel. If our conclusion makes anyone wonder whether they are saved because they are not in a group, we have preached another gospel (Gal 1:8).

**The resolution.** Discipleship is the *shape* of the Christian life, not the *price* of it. Union with Christ brings justification and sanctification together and inseparably: Christ became to us "righteousness and sanctification and redemption" (1 Cor 1:30); those united to his death are united to his life (Rom 6:5); "he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion" (Phil 1:6). Faith that justifies is never alone (Jas 2:17). So growth is the *evidence and fruit* of grace, never its purchase price (Gal 2:20; Titus 2:11–14).

**And nobody signs up.** The framing of the objection is itself off. "You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit" (John 15:16). Discipleship is not an opt-in program for the committed; it is what being called by Christ is. A member who has never been in a group has not declined a tier — he has been under-served, and Scripture locates that failure with those who should have fed him: "by this time you ought to be teachers" is a rebuke, and the letter is addressed to a congregation and its leaders (Heb 5:12; cf. Judg 2:10; Ezek 34:4).

**The two failure modes to avoid.** Treating discipleship as a condition of salvation is legalism and produces exactly the anxiety Galatians was written against (Gal 3:3; 4:9–10). Treating it as optional is the cheap grace that leaves people fed for decades and reproducing nothing (Jas 1:22). Both are live in a conservative church, and the second is often the price of over-correcting the first.

**Author's rating: firm, and it governs how any proposal is presented.** No initiative may be framed as sorting real Christians from nominal ones. It is not a filter; it is feeding what grace has already begun. Practical test: would a member who never joins anything hear our proposal as a question about his salvation? If so, we have framed it wrongly, whatever the content.

### C7. "What are we aiming at? A definition of maturity."

**Why it belongs here.** Every position in this document drives toward "mature and equipped," and the phrase is useless until it is defined. Without a definition, the question "are our people mature?" cannot be answered, and any model gets to define its target after the fact.

**Scripture's own markers.** Paul's stated goal is "to present everyone mature in Christ" (Col 1:28), and he unpacks it: attaining "to the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, to mature manhood, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ," so as to be no longer children tossed by every wind of doctrine, but "speaking the truth in love" and growing up into Christ, "when each part is working properly" (Eph 4:13–16). Hebrews adds a functional test: the mature are those "who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil" (5:14) — trained by practice, not by information. Peter adds that growth is in "grace and knowledge" together (2 Pet 3:18), and John that the test is love of the brothers (1 John 3:14).

**A working definition.** A mature believer knows the faith well enough not to be moved by novelty, discerns good from evil by trained practice rather than by rule-following, tells the truth in love, works properly as a part of the body, and is able to feed someone else (Heb 5:12). Doctrine, discernment, character, function, reproduction.

**Author's rating: firm enough to use, and it should be stated before any proposal.** Note what it does to the debate: three of the five markers cannot be assessed from attendance records, and none can be produced by a curriculum alone.

### C8. "If attendance is the measure, name the elephant: they met daily, and we do not."

**Case.** The means-centered position holds that faithful attendance at worship and teaching is a sufficient discipleship plan. That claim should be tested against the frequency Scripture actually describes. The Jerusalem church continued "day by day, attending the temple together and breaking bread in their homes" (Acts 2:46). The apostles taught "every day, in the temple and from house to house" (Acts 5:42). Paul taught "in public and from house to house" (Acts 20:20). And most importantly, Hebrews commands it: "exhort one another *every day*, as long as it is called 'today,' that none of you may be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin" (3:13).

Set that beside current practice. Three in five U.S. Protestant pastors now consider someone a regular churchgoer at twice a month or more, with a plurality setting the threshold at exactly twice monthly (Lifeway Research, 2023). Whatever our own rolls say, the category "regular attender" has quietly moved from daily to fortnightly.

So the argument is this: the position that attendance is sufficient is defending a frequency the New Testament church would not recognize as contact at all. If contact is what forms disciples, we have roughly two percent of the contact the Jerusalem church had. That is the gap the sufficiency claim has to answer.

**Counter — three of them, and they matter.**

*First, frequency is not the same as life on life.* A church could meet daily for lectures and still leave every one-another command unperformed. More Sundays is not more discipleship. This argument proves that our contact is thin; it does not by itself prove that a structured relational model is the remedy.

*Second, Acts 2:46 is narrative, and the framework in section A applies to it like everything else.* Daily temple gathering is not commanded, and the conditions were unrepeatable: one city, a temple courtyard available, thousands of recent converts, and a community scattered by persecution within six chapters (Acts 8:1). Under our own three bins, daily gathering is commended, not commanded. If we press it as binding, we have abandoned the method we adopted.

*Third, Acts 5:42 and Acts 20:20 describe apostles teaching.* They support intensive officer-led ministry, including house-to-house visitation, which is the means-centered position's own historic practice. They do not by themselves establish every-member work.

**Rejoinder.** Grant all three, and one text survives untouched: **Hebrews 3:13.** It is a precept, not a narrative. It is addressed to a congregation, not to officers. It uses reciprocal language — *one another*. It carries an explicit frequency — *every day*. And it states its purpose, which is precisely formation and preservation: that none be hardened by sin's deceit. That is not a description of the Jerusalem church's unusual circumstances. It is a standing command to a church, and it cannot be discharged by an order of worship.

Note what that does. The frequency argument's real force is not that it makes daily gathering binding. It is that it dismantles the sufficiency claim from the inside. Whoever says attendance is enough must explain how a congregation meeting twice a month obeys a command to exhort one another every day. There are only three answers: that the command is not for us, that it is discharged some other way, or that we are not obeying it. The first is untenable; the second requires naming the other way, which is the whole question; the third is the finding.

And observe where the daily contact in Acts actually happened: *in their homes*, house to house (2:46; 5:42; 20:20). The New Testament's high-frequency setting is domestic, not institutional. That points at households and ordinary hospitality before it points at any curriculum (see D4).

**Author's rating: firm that the sufficiency claim cannot survive Heb 3:13. Contested whether it favors any particular model.** It closes the door on "what we are doing is enough." It does not open a door to any program.

### C9. "What does *baptizing* actually mean here?"

**Why this needs care.** The word is read three ways, and two of them mislead. Some hear "baptizing" and conclude the Commission is discharged by administering ordinances — sacraments plus preaching, duty done. Others, usually from outside our tradition, treat it loosely as evangelism, "helping people identify with Christ," with the ordinance incidental. And there is a third possibility worth taking seriously: that Matthew is describing what baptism *effects* — incorporation into the triune name — rather than prescribing a ritual act as the disciple-maker's personal task.

**Start where our standards are firm.** Baptism is the sacrament. It was "ordained by Jesus Christ, not only for the solemn admission of the party baptized into the visible church; but also to be unto him a sign and seal of the covenant of grace, of his ingrafting into Christ, of regeneration, of remission of sins, and of his giving up unto God... to walk in newness of life" (WCF 28.1); water, "administered by a minister of the gospel, lawfully called thereunto" (28.2). WSC 94 calls it "our engagement to be the Lord's." The loose "identify with Christ" reading is not our position and should be set aside plainly.

**But the sacrament is never bare, and our own standards say so.** "There is in every sacrament a spiritual relation, or sacramental union, between the sign and the thing signified; whence it comes to pass that the names and effects of the one are attributed to the other" (WCF 27.2). So naming the sign names the reality. When Matthew writes *baptizing them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*, the weight is not on a ceremony performed but on a person transferred. The preposition is *eis* — into — with a single *onoma* over three persons: the language of passing into another's possession and allegiance.

**Three textual pressures that support reading it this way.**

*First, the apostles did not treat it as a formula.* Acts baptizes "in the name of Jesus Christ" (2:38), "in the name of the Lord Jesus" (8:16; 19:5 — the Greek preposition is *eis*, so "into" is a fair rendering, but the KJV reads "in" at all four), "in the name of Jesus Christ" (10:48). The triune wording of Matt 28:19 appears nowhere else in the New Testament. (To be clear: no Greek manuscript omits it, and the argument that it is a later insertion rests on a few Eusebian citations against unanimous manuscript evidence. That road is closed.) The sound conclusion is not that the words are spurious but that Matthew is naming what baptism is *into*, not dictating what must be said over the water.

*Second, Paul says his commission was not baptizing.* "Christ did not send me to baptize but to preach the gospel" (1 Cor 1:17), and he could barely remember whom he had baptized (1:14–16). Paul is an apostle operating under this very Commission. If *baptizontes* named the disciple-maker's central personal task, that sentence would be very strange. It is not strange if the participle describes what must happen *to* those made disciples, which the church administers through its officers.

*Third, the placement is structural.* Baptism stands between going and teaching-to-keep. It is the boundary crossing — the point at which a person is publicly transferred, admitted to the visible church, and placed under its care and its discipline.

**What follows, and it cuts both ways.**

*Against a careless every-member argument:* the sacrament belongs to office. A member may not administer it. So anyone arguing that disciple-making belongs to every member must say that it does not belong to every member in every part. This should be said plainly, and first.

*Against the sufficiency claim:* if what baptism does is place a person inside a body that now owes him something, then citing "baptizing" as evidence that the Commission is discharged by administering ordinances proves the opposite. Baptism is once; teaching-to-keep is lifelong and aimed at *the baptized*. A baptized member whom no one knows, corrects, or restores has been admitted to a care he is not receiving.

**Author's rating: firm on the confessional definition. Legitimately open on emphasis** — whether Matthew stresses the rite or what the rite effects. Either way the conclusion above holds, so we need not settle it to proceed. What we must not do is let "baptizing" be read as *ceremony administered, obligation discharged*.

### C10. "It does not say *teaching them everything I taught you*. It says *teaching them to observe*."

**Case.** The Greek is *didaskontes autous tērein panta hosa eneteilamēn hymin* — teaching them **to keep** all that I have **commanded** you. Two words carry the weight, and both are routinely softened in how the verse is used.

The verb of content is *eneteilamēn*, from *entellomai* — to command, to give orders. Not *edidaxa*, "I taught." The subject matter of the Commission's teaching is not the body of instruction Jesus delivered; it is the set of commands he issued. That difference decides what counts as success. If the subject matter were his teaching, a well-catechized member who obeys little has been given what the verse requires. It is not, so he has not.

And the infinitive is *tērein* — keep, guard, obey. It states what *didaskontes* teaches: the thing being taught is *keeping*. Obedience is not the hoped-for result of the teaching; obedience is its content. The same verb runs through John: "if you love me, you will keep my commandments" (14:15); "whoever says 'I know him' but does not keep his commandments is a liar" (1 John 2:3–4). James states the failure mode directly — hearers who are not doers deceive themselves (1:22). Hebrews states the method — powers of discernment "trained by constant practice" (5:14).

**Why this is not cognitive.** No one is taught to *do* a thing purely by being told it. This is why Paul's summary has four parts, not one: "what you have learned and received and heard and *seen in me* — practice these things" (Phil 4:9; cf. 3:17; 1 Cor 11:1; 2 Tim 3:10). Three of the four require presence. It is also why *teaching to observe* implies someone positioned to notice whether observing is happening. A command taught with no one watching for obedience is a command taught as information.

**Counter.** Preaching aims at obedience too; the point is not unique to relational ministry. A faithful pulpit applies the text to the conscience and calls for doing, and the Spirit — not a mentor — produces obedience. Pressed too hard, this argument implies preaching that does not produce visible obedience is defective preaching, which would indict Isaiah and Jeremiah.

**Rejoinder.** Granted on both. Preaching does aim at obedience, and fruit is the Spirit's to give. The claim is narrower: that the Commission's verb requires *someone to be teaching someone to keep*, that keeping is visible, and that visibility requires proximity. A pulpit can command obedience to a thousand people. It cannot teach one of them to keep, in the sense of watching, correcting, and returning — which is the ordinary human way anyone learns to do anything. Isaiah's hearers were not obeying, but Isaiah knew that; the prophet was not insulated from the result. Our arrangement can be.

**Author's rating: firm, and this is the second-strongest grammatical argument in the paper after C1.** It is a precept, not a narrative inference, so it survives every hermeneutical objection in section A.

### C11. "Does *poreuthentes* mean 'as you go' or 'go'? And does it mean leaning outward always?"

**The first question is settled, and not in favor of the popular reading.** "As you go, make disciples" is a fixture of preaching and it is a grammatical error. The form *poreuthentes* occurs seven times in Matthew and fifteen times in the New Testament, and in none of them does it mean "as" or "while you go" — it is consistently a command to go, coupled to a following imperative: go and search for the child (2:8), go and learn what this means (9:13), go and tell John (11:4), go and tell his disciples (28:7). Wallace's rule is that virtually every aorist participle followed by an aorist imperative in narrative is attendant circumstance (*Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics*, 640–645), and he notes that in Matthew specifically, every other instance of the aorist participle of "go" before a main verb is clearly attendant circumstance. If the sense were "as you are going," Matthew had a present participle available and did not use it.

**So going is commanded.** The participle picks up the imperative's mood. That is the point worth making to a session: going is not a soft frame around the real command; it shares the command's force, and it appears on no list of the ordinary means of grace.

**The second question needs a more careful answer than the first.** "Leaning outward all the time" is a true statement about the Christian life, but it cannot be extracted from this participle, and a session should not be told that it can. The aorist views the going as a whole, undefined action; it does not depict an ongoing posture. If anything the aspect runs the other way — *poreuthentes* is aorist while *baptizontes* and *didaskontes* are present, so the picture is a decisive going with ongoing baptizing and teaching following from it. Attendant circumstance also means the participle remains subordinate in emphasis: "make disciples" is still the main verb. Going is commanded, but it is not the center of gravity.

**Where the outward lean legitimately comes from.** From the scope — *panta ta ethnē*, all the nations, which cannot be reached by a stationary church. From the parallel commissions: "as the Father has sent me, even so I am sending you" (John 20:21); "you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). From the pattern in Acts, where the church is sent and receives the report (13:1–3; 14:26–27). And from the Gospels' own sending of the Twelve and the seventy-two before any of this. The doctrine is well supported. It simply rests on those texts, not on the tense of one participle.

**Author's rating: firm that "as you go" is wrong and that going is commanded. Firm that the perpetual-outward-posture conclusion is true but is carried by scope and by parallel texts, not by this participle's aspect.**

## D. Who does it

### C12. "Scripture commands formation by observed example."

**Case.** This position matters most because of where it sits. The epistles command Christians to be formed by watching particular people live, and command some to arrange their lives so as to be watched. Hebrews 13:7 — "Remember them which have the rule over you, who have spoken unto you the word of God: whose faith follow, **considering the end of their conversation**." Their speaking is named; their conduct is named separately, as its own object of attention. Philippians 3:17 — "be followers together of me, and **mark them which walk so** as ye have us for an ensample" — where the verb is *skopeite*, to observe closely, and the people to be observed are not apostles but ordinary believers in the congregation. Second Thessalonians 3:7–9 — Paul worked with his hands though he had every right not to, expressly "to make ourselves an ensample unto you to follow us." He arranged his life to be copied. Add 1 Corinthians 4:16–17, where Timothy is sent to remind them of Paul's *ways*, not merely his doctrine, and Hebrews 6:12.

**Why it changes the shape of the case.** Everything else we have about presence and modeling sits in the "What Jesus did" column, which an elder may contest position by position. These texts move the same claim into Precept. They hold for a man who reads Jesus' training of the Twelve as no template for a congregation — and they answer the concern that building on Jesus' ministry is the error, from the letters themselves.

**Counter.** Michaelis (*TDNT* 4:659–674) reads *mimētēs* in Paul as obedience to apostolic teaching rather than copying a life. This is a live scholarly position with a real history — see also D. M. Stanley, *Biblica* 40 (1959), and De Boer, *The Imitation of Paul* (1962) — and it is the counter a careful reader will reach for.

**Rejoinder.** State Michaelis and do not dismiss him. Then note the three texts that resist reduction to "obey what we taught": Hebrews 13:7 specifies *the end of their conversation*, Philippians 3:17 uses a verb of visual observation, and 2 Thessalonians 3:7–9 describes Paul deliberately structuring his conduct so it could be copied. The entry claims those three and no more.

**Author's rating: firm, and the most valuable single addition to the case.**

### C13. "The goal is every individual mature — and Paul says so against an inner circle."

**Case.** Colossians 1:28: "warning **every man**, and teaching **every man** in all wisdom; that we may present **every man** perfect in Christ Jesus." *Panta anthrōpon*, three times in one sentence. We have been using this verse for the content of maturity (C7) and leaving its most striking feature unused. The goal named is not a mature congregation in aggregate; it is each individual, presented complete.

**The double edge, which is the more useful half.** Lightfoot reads the threefold repetition as polemical: the Colossian errorists "sought to form an inner mystical school or circle of discipleship within the Church, initiated into a wisdom and holiness supposed to be higher than that attainable by ordinary Christian faith." Paul's *every man, every man, every man* is aimed at exactly that. So this verse supports the case's central claim — every member formed, individually — and simultaneously moves the guard against two tiers from prudence to exegesis. We have been arguing against inner rings on grounds of wisdom. Colossians 1:28 argues against them on the ground of Paul's stated purpose in a letter written against that very error.

**Counter.** The "we" of the verse is Paul and his co-workers. It establishes the goal, not the agency.

**Rejoinder.** Conceded, because C13 is doing work on the *what* axis rather than the *who*. The warning that matters runs the other way: this verse does not justify concentrating on a few. In Lightfoot's reading it cuts against that.

**Author's rating: firm, and it strengthens the guard against two tiers.**

### C14. "An apostle's summary of his own ministry is individual, sustained and costly."

**Case.** Acts 20:31 — "by the space of three years I ceased not to warn **every one** night and day with tears." We use Acts 20:20 (publicly and from house to house) and have left the stronger verse eleven lines later unused. *Hena hekaston*: each one individually. Then duration, frequency and cost, all specified — three years, night and day, with tears. The verb is *noutheteō*, the same word as Colossians 1:28. Asked to summarize three years of ministry, Paul does not say he preached faithfully, though he had. He says he warned each person, continuously, at personal cost.

**Counter.** This is Paul describing apostolic ministry, to elders. It belongs on the officers' side of the *who* question and says nothing about every-member discipling.

**Rejoinder.** The counter is right, and this entry is included **because** it strengthens the other view's half of the argument. If the case for every-member discipling is sound it does not need this verse. A fair paper files each text where it falls, and C14 makes the elders' obligation heavier.

**Author's rating: firm, and it lands on the officers' side.**

### D5. "A whole congregation of ordinary believers became the example to others."

**Case.** This supplies what C1f could not, where the Acts 13 sending turned out to commission apostles rather than ordinary members. First Thessalonians 1:6–8 gives the chain outright: "ye became followers of us, and of the Lord" (v. 6) — "**so that ye were ensamples to all that believe** in Macedonia and Achaia" (v. 7) — "for **from you sounded out the word of the Lord**... in every place your faith to God-ward is spread abroad" (v. 8). **Counter.** It is narrative description, not command. Paul reports what happened; he does not instruct them to reproduce.

**Rejoinder.** Conceded, and the limit should be stated wherever the entry is used. D5 establishes that reproduction by ordinary congregations is normal and commendable. It does not establish that every member is required to be discipling someone.

### C15. "Restoration is aimed at strengthening others."

**Case.** Luke 22:31–32: "Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat: but I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not: **and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren**." The recovery is given a purpose, and the purpose is other people. Falling, being restored, and then strengthening others is one motion, with the last stated as the object of the first two.

**Why it matters more than it looks.** A. B. Bruce, commenting on this verse, writes that "Jesus expects the frail disciple to become strong in grace, and so able and willing to help the weak," and — this is the load-bearing clause — "He cherishes this expectation **with respect to all**, but specially in regard to Peter." A Free Church professor of New Testament exegesis generalizing the expectation from Peter to every disciple is a Reformed voice on the *who* question, where the positions are most evenly divided. It also supplies the link C1e was missing: correction and restoration are not terminal, they are aimed at reproduction.

**Counter.** It is said to Peter, about a member of the apostolic band, before Pentecost. The generalization to every disciple is Bruce's inference and not the text's plain statement.

**Rejoinder.** The generalization is Bruce's, not the verse's. The text says it to Peter. Bruce argues it holds of all, and he wrote long before this discussion and has no stake in it.

**Author's rating: firm on Bruce's reading; state whose reading it is.**

### D6. "The same commission was given twice, and the second group was not the Twelve."

**Case.** This position bears on the *who* question. It is structural rather than normative: it defeats an objection instead of making a positive claim.

The standing objection to every Gospel argument in this paper is that the Twelve were apostles, that their formation was apostolic, and that nothing transfers. We concede that repeatedly and rightly. **But the objection quarantines the Twelve, and Jesus did not.** Luke 10:1 — "the Lord appointed **other** seventy also, and sent them two and two before his face." *Heterous*: expressly a different group, anonymous, far larger, holding no continuing office and listed nowhere.

What they receive is the Twelve's commission point for point. Sent in pairs (Luke 10:1; Mark 6:7). Instructed before going (10:2–12; cf. Matt 10:5–15). Told to heal and announce the kingdom (10:9; cf. Luke 9:2). Returned and reported (10:17; cf. Mark 6:30). Corrected on return — rejoice not that the spirits are subject, but that your names are written in heaven (10:20). And most tellingly the identical formula of representative authority: to the Twelve, "he that receiveth you receiveth me, and he that receiveth me receiveth him that sent me" (Matt 10:40); to the seventy, "he that heareth you heareth me; and he that despiseth you despiseth me; and he that despiseth me despiseth him that sent me" (Luke 10:16). The same three-link chain, to a group that was not the apostolic band.

The harvest saying brackets both missions — spoken before the Twelve go (Matt 9:37–38) and to the seventy as they go (Luke 10:2). The prayer for more labourers is put into the labourers' mouths at the moment of sending.

**Counter.** The seventy were commissioned directly by Christ, returned reporting authority over demons, and were sent on a specific preparatory errand "before his face into every city and place, whither he himself would come." Luke alone records it, and the manuscripts divide between seventy and seventy-two.

**Rejoinder.** Concede every word of that, and notice that none of it touches the claim. The argument is about **scope**, not about normativity: an elder may still contest which parts of Jesus' practice bind — that is A2's question and this entry does not settle it — but he can no longer say the pattern was apostolic *in extent*. Demonstrably it was not. Whether seventy or seventy-two, nothing turns on the number.

**One honest note.** Bruce's thirty-one chapters contain none on the seventy; they appear in a preface footnote about Baur and nowhere else. That is a real limitation of leaning on him as the Reformed precedent, since his subject is precisely the narrower body that the other view calls unrepeatable. The argument does not depend on him.

**Author's rating: firm that the pattern was not apostolic in scope; silent on normativity.**

### C16. "Our own constitution assigns these duties to private Christians first, and to elders especially."

**Case.** Read BCO 8-3 past the sentence everyone stops at. It begins with oversight and discipline, then visiting the people at their homes, instructing the ignorant, comforting the mourner, nourishing and guarding the children. Then it says what kind of duties these are: "All those duties which private Christians are bound to discharge by the law of love are especially incumbent upon them by divine vocation, and are to be discharged as official duties." Read it slowly. The constitution does not say these are elder duties that private Christians may also do. It says they are private Christians' duties — bound on every believer by the law of love — which elders are *especially* bound to, and must discharge officially. The baseline is every member. The officer's distinction is intensity and accountability, not category.

The same paragraph, in its current form, names the content: elders are to set an example to the flock "by their zeal to evangelize the unconverted, make disciples, and demonstrate hospitality," and to "pray with and for the people, being careful and diligent in seeking the fruit of the preached Word among the flock." Three things to notice. *Make disciples* is named as an elder duty by example, in our own polity, in those words. *Evangelize the unconverted* sits beside it. And the last clause assumes that preaching the Word and seeking its fruit are two different things, and assigns the second to someone.

Alongside it: BCO 12-5.d charges every session "to promote obedience to the Great Commission in its totality at home and abroad" — *in its totality* — and the first principle under BCO 14-1 reads simply that the Church is responsible for carrying out the Great Commission. BCO 4-4 lists prayer first among the ordinances Christ established.

**Counter.** The "private Christians" sentence is about the *law of love* — general Christian charity — not about disciple-making specifically. Every Christian must comfort the mourner; it does not follow that every Christian is a disciple-maker. And 8-3 was amended in 2019; the "make disciples" wording may be recent, which cuts against treating it as the settled mind of the tradition.

**Rejoinder.** Both points are fair and neither removes the argument. On the first: the sentence sits immediately after a list that includes *instruct the ignorant* and *nourish and guard the children of the Church* — which are formation, not only charity — and immediately before *make disciples* is named outright. The law of love in 8-3 is doing discipling work. On the second: that 8-3 was amended in 2019 means the PCA, acting as a General Assembly, chose these words recently and deliberately. That is not a weakness. It is the denomination's current, considered position, and the other reading has to argue against it.

**Author's rating: firm, and it is the first position in this column that places the duty with every member.** This is the PCA's own constitution placing the baseline with private Christians.

### C17. "The ordinary means are three, not two. A model built on Word and sacrament has dropped one."

**Case.** The alternative view describes discipleship as Word and sacrament, received. That is a two-item list, and it is one item short of the catechism it appeals to. WSC 88 names the outward and ordinary means as "his ordinances, especially the Word, sacraments, and prayer," and WLC 154 says the same. Prayer is not an accompaniment to the means of grace. It is one of them, named in the same breath, by the same standard, with the same adjective — *ordinary*. Our polity agrees: BCO 4-4 lists the ordinances Christ established in his Church and puts prayer first; BCO 8-3 charges elders to "pray with and for the people." So a church that measures formation by attendance on Word and sacrament has, by its own confession, left out a third of the ordinary means. That is not a criticism imported from outside the tradition. It is the tradition's own arithmetic.

**Counter.** Nobody disputes that prayer is a means of grace. Every service has public prayer (BCO 52), and the catechism's "prayer" in WSC 88 means prayer offered in the ordinary assemblies of the church, not a program. So the triad is already honoured on Sunday morning, and this argument proves nothing about what a congregation is missing.

**Rejoinder.** The counter is right that WSC 88 does not specify a program, and the paper should not claim it does. But it misreads what the argument is for. The argument does not say prayer is absent from worship. It says the alternative *definition* is two-thirds of the confession's definition — and that a definition that drops prayer will build a model that measures prayer nowhere. Whether a particular church has in fact let prayer thin out is an empirical question that church must answer about itself; this argument only establishes that if it has, it has departed from its own standard, not merely from a preference.

**Author's rating: firm as a confessional point; proves nothing about any particular congregation.** It is made from WSC 88, the very text the other view cites, so it asks nothing beyond our own standards.

### C18. "Prayer is the one thing the disciples asked to be taught, and the way Christ and the apostles formed people."

**Case.** Across four Gospels there is one recorded occasion on which the disciples ask Jesus, in those words, to teach them something: "Lord, teach us to pray" (Luke 11:1). They ask him to explain parables (Matt 13:36) and to tell them when things will happen (Matt 24:3); they never ask to be taught to preach. The one *teach us* is prayer. The request comes after watching him pray; the formation was by observation before it was by instruction. And Christ's own forming of his disciples ran through prayer at every decisive point — all night before choosing them (Luke 6:12), interceding for Peter before his fall so that he could strengthen others after it (Luke 22:32), and the long prayer of John 17 in which he does not instruct them but prays for them, and then for everyone who would believe through them. When they fail, he traces the failure to prayerlessness (Mark 9:29).

The apostles then make prayer the first-named thing. "We will give ourselves continually to prayer, and to the ministry of the word" (Acts 6:4) — prayer before Word, in a sentence written to describe what the office is for. And Paul reports, with approval, a man who was not an apostle doing formation by prayer: Epaphras, "one of you," "always labouring fervently for you in prayers, that ye may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God" (Col 4:12). The word is *teleioi*, the same word as Colossians 1:28 — Paul's own goal for *every man* — and here an ordinary member is labouring toward it for others, on his knees.

**And prayer is where mission begins.** Before the Twelve are sent, and again before the seventy, the first instruction is the same: "Pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest, that he will send forth labourers into his harvest" (Matt 9:38; Luke 10:2). The sending is preceded by praying for sending. This is why mission and prayer belong together on any list of what a model must contain: in the Gospels they are one movement, and the first half of it is prayer.

**Counter.** All of this establishes that prayer matters, which no one denies, and that Jesus and Paul prayed, which no one disputes. It does not establish that prayer is a *means of making disciples* as distinct from a general Christian duty, and the Epaphras verse is a greeting, not a precept.

**Rejoinder.** Fair on the Epaphras verse: it is description, not command, and should be weighed as that — though Paul's choice to report it, and to specify its aim as the maturity of others, is not nothing. The precept weight is carried by Acts 6:4 and Matthew 9:38, which are commands about what the apostles would do and what the sent must first do. And the distinction the counter draws — prayer as general duty versus prayer as formation — is one Scripture does not draw. Colossians 4:12 and Luke 22:32 both describe prayer *aimed at someone else's formation*. That is the specific claim, and it is in the text.

**Author's rating: firm on the Gospel and apostolic pattern; the precept weight rests on Acts 6:4 and Matthew 9:38.** Epaphras is the ordinary-member instance, not the ground.

### C19. "Yes, that was Jesus. And here is each of the six done by human hands, and commanded of ordinary believers."

**The objection, stated fairly.** C1a to C1f describe what a disciple received from Jesus. The reply is simple and serious: *that was Jesus.* The Son of God, who knew what was in man (John 2:25), who chose with foreknowledge (John 6:70), who was without sin. No church member can call, keep, teach, cost, restore and send the way he did. So the six describe the incarnation, not a job description, and nothing in them transfers to a human discipler.

**Case.** The objection would be decisive if the New Testament left the six with Jesus. It does not. For every one of them Scripture shows a human doing it, and for every one it commands ordinary believers to do it.

- **Attached to a named teacher (C1a).** Paul calls Timothy "my own son in the faith" (1 Tim 1:2), and reminds him, "thou hast fully known my doctrine, manner of life, purpose, faith" (2 Tim 3:10) — attachment to a person, by name. And the command: "mark them which walk so as ye have us for an ensample" (Phil 3:17); "whose faith follow, considering the end of their conversation" (Heb 13:7).
- **Kept with him (C1b).** "We were willing to have imparted unto you, not the gospel of God only, but also our own souls" (1 Thess 2:8); three years "night and day" (Acts 20:31). The command: the aged women are to teach the young women (Titus 2:3–4) — formation that assumes shared life; "support the weak, be patient toward all" (1 Thess 5:14).
- **Taught toward obedience (C1c).** "Publickly, and from house to house" (Acts 20:20). The command, to the whole congregation: "teaching and admonishing one another" (Col 3:16); "exhort one another daily" (Heb 3:13).
- **Called to cost (C1d).** "Endure hardness, as a good soldier" (2 Tim 2:3), from a man who had listed his own persecutions in the verses before (2 Tim 3:10–12). The command: "consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works" (Heb 10:24).
- **Restored after failure (C1e).** Paul directs the Corinthians to forgive and comfort the repentant man "lest he be swallowed up with overmuch sorrow" (2 Cor 2:7). The command — and note to whom: "Brethren... ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness" (Gal 6:1); "if any of you do err from the truth, and one convert him" (Jas 5:19); "go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone" (Matt 18:15), addressed to any brother.
- **Sent, and the report taken (C1f).** Barnabas and Paul "gathered the church together, and rehearsed all that God had done with them" (Acts 14:27); "let us go again and visit our brethren... and see how they do" (Acts 15:36). The command and the ordinary instance: a congregation sends Epaphroditus, "your messenger" (Phil 2:25); every disciple is told to pray the Lord of the harvest to send labourers (Matt 9:38; Luke 10:2).

**And the bridge is a single verse.** "Be ye followers of me, even as I also am of Christ" (1 Cor 11:1). The objection says Christ's example cannot reach an ordinary believer because the gap is too wide. Paul says it reaches them *through a human* — himself — and names that as the design. The human discipler is not a substitute for Christ's example. He is the ordinary means by which it arrives.

**Counter.** Paul was an apostle, so most of the human instances above are apostolic too; the argument has moved the quarantine line, not removed it. And the commands to one another (Col 3:16, Heb 3:13, Gal 6:1) are general Christian duties, not a mandate for structured disciple-making.

**Rejoinder.** Both fair, and both answered within the list. The human *instances* are mostly apostolic, which is why each line carries a *command* as well — and the commands are to brethren, aged women, "ye which are spiritual," the whole church at Colossae. Titus 2 and Gal 6:1 are addressed to people who held no office. On the second point: the paper claims nothing structured. It claims that the six things a disciple received are things ordinary believers are told to give one another. What shape that takes is prudence (bin three). That they are to be given at all is precept.

**State the limit, because it is real.** What does not transfer is everything that belonged to Christ as Christ: knowledge of hearts, foreknowledge in choosing, sinlessness, the authority to forgive. A human does the six fallibly, under grace (C6), under the oversight of elders (C16), and sometimes badly. That is a reason for oversight. It is not a reason to leave the six undone.

**Author's rating: firm, and it is the answer to the objection most likely to be raised against C1a–C1f.**

### D1. "Disciple-making is reserved to officers."

**Case.** Christ gave shepherds who will give account (Acts 20:28; Heb 13:17; 1 Pet 5:1–4). Teaching carries stricter judgment (Jas 3:1). An elder must hold firm the trustworthy word and rebuke those who contradict it (Titus 1:9). Unaccountable spiritual authority produced Korah (Num 16).

**Counter.** The commands that constitute discipleship are addressed to congregations: older women are to *teach* what is good and train the young women (Titus 2:3–5); the church is to be "teaching and admonishing one another" (Col 3:16), to "exhort one another every day" (Heb 3:13), to be "able to instruct one another" (Rom 15:14), to restore and bear burdens (Gal 6:1–2), to use each gift "to serve one another" (1 Pet 4:10–11). Hebrews rebukes a congregation because "you ought to be teachers" (5:12). The scattered — not the apostles — preached first beyond Jerusalem (Acts 8:4). Priscilla and Aquila, tentmakers, explained the way of God more accurately to Apollos (Acts 18:26).

**Rejoinder.** None of those texts creates an office or authorizes teaching apart from oversight. "Every member" and "under officers" are compatible.

**Author's rating: firm that members disciple; contested where the line falls.** The live dispute is not whether but where mutual admonition ends and the teaching office begins — and no text draws that line precisely.

### D2. "Eph 4:12 proves every-member ministry."

**Case.** Officers equip the saints *for* the work of ministry; grace was given to each one (4:7); the body grows when each part is working properly (4:16).

**Counter.** The reading turns on grammar, and the grammar is disputed on two points. First, syntax: the verse has three prepositional phrases — *pros* the completing of the saints, *eis* the work of ministry, *eis* the building up of the body. The modern reading treats the change of preposition as marking a change of subject, so that officers equip and then *the saints* do the ministry. The older reading takes all three as coordinate purpose statements modifying "he gave," so that all three describe the officers' own work. Nothing in the Greek requires the first reading; the preposition shift is suggestive, not decisive. Second, lexis: *katartismos* appears only here in the New Testament, and its verb means to mend nets (Matt 4:21), to restore a brother (Gal 6:1), to make complete (Heb 13:21). If the sense is "make whole" rather than "train for a task," the every-member reading loses its footing in this verse.

**Rejoinder.** The dispute is confined to v. 12. Verse 7 says grace was given to *each one*, and v. 16 says the body builds itself up "when each part is working properly" — neither is syntactically contested, and both make ministry the work of members. So even conceding v. 12 entirely, the paragraph still teaches what the verse is usually cited to prove.

**Author's rating: contested — do not build on this verse alone.** Titus 2:3–5, Col 3:16, and Heb 3:13 carry the case without a syntactical fight.

### D4. "Discipline and households are the instruments we already have."

**Church discipline.** The confession names discipline among Christ's ordinances, and our polity provides for it. It is the church's ordained instrument for confronting sin in a member — which is the very function accountability groups are asked to perform. Matt 18:15–17 puts the first step in a member's hands ("go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone") and escalates to the church. Gal 6:1 assigns restoration to "you who are spiritual." Before proposing new structures for accountability, a session should ask whether the ordained one is functioning. If it is not, that comes first.

**Households.** The most obvious every-member disciple-making in any congregation is parents with their children. It is explicitly commanded (Deut 6:6–9; Eph 6:4), it is the one arrangement that already exists in most homes, it requires no new program, no cohort, and no curriculum purchase, and it is where the failure of Judg 2:10 actually happens. Timothy's faith came from a grandmother and a mother before it came from Paul (2 Tim 1:5). Our own tradition assumes it: in August 1647 the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland approved a Directory for Family Worship, the same month it adopted the Westminster Confession.

**Author's rating: firm, and this is the most actionable thing in the whole discussion.** If the session wants to obey the every-member conclusion without adopting any model at all, equipping parents and strengthening discipline would do more, sooner, at less cost, and with less risk of two-tier drift, than any group structure. Any proposal that skips past these to a curriculum has skipped the near thing for the far one.

## E. Structure and models

### E1. "A structured model (curriculum, groups, accountability) is required."

**Case.** Intentionality is biblical: Jesus chose after a night of prayer (Luke 6:12–13); Paul planned itineraries, appointed elders city by city (Acts 14:23), and left Titus in Crete to put what remained in order (Titus 1:5). What is not planned does not happen, and leaving formation to prudence leaves it to chance (Jas 1:22).

**Counter.** No model is commanded. Where Scripture is silent on form, conscience is free — "who are you to pass judgment on the servant of another?" (Rom 14:4); "for freedom Christ has set us free" (Gal 5:1); "let no one disqualify you" over self-made regulations with an appearance of wisdom (Col 2:16–23). Requiring a form Scripture does not require is the Galatian error.

**Rejoinder.** Advocating a model is not binding it; a session may adopt a form as wisdom without requiring it of consciences. And the freedom argument should not be applied selectively: no preaching schedule, membership class, or order of service is commanded either, and nobody calls those Galatian. The warning applies where merit is being claimed, not wherever structure exists.

**Author's rating: firm against "required."** The strongest form of the pro-structure case is "wise and worth adopting." No named model can be shown to be commanded.

*Column: Prudence · Use: Lead*

### E2. "A model like TEAMS is not an arbitrary program; it is an attempt to codify what the Gospels put into the word 'disciple.'"

**Case.** This is the model's real claim, and it deserves to be stated at full strength. If C1 is right — that "make disciples" carries the content the Gospels spent three years loading into the term — then obedience requires delivering that content, and a church needs some way of knowing whether it is. TEAMS is offered as that inventory, not as an innovation. Test it against the Gospels' own material:

- **Truth** — he taught them, publicly and privately (Matt 5:2; 13:36; Mark 4:34).
- **Equipping** — appointed to be with him, and "when fully trained will be like his teacher" (Mark 3:14; Luke 6:40).
- **Accountability** — he corrected them: rebuking Peter (Matt 16:23), redirecting the seventy-two's joy (Luke 10:20), restoring Peter after failure (John 21:15–19).
- **Mission** — he sent them and received their report (Luke 9:1–10; 10:1–24).
- **Supplication** — he prayed all night before choosing them, and prayed for them by name (Luke 6:12; 22:32; John 17).

On that showing the acronym is not a technique laid over Scripture; it is a summary of Scripture's own content. That is a far better argument than any appeal to results, and it is the argument to make.

**Counter.** A codification is only as good as its mapping, and this one has gaps — offered as improvements, not as disqualifications, since a mnemonic is not a doctrinal statement. The most prominent element in the Gospels' definition of a disciple is *cost* — deny yourself and take up your cross (Luke 9:23), addressed to crowds, twice, at length (Luke 14:25–33; Matt 10:37–39). TEAMS has no letter for it. Two smaller omissions. *Presence* is assumed by the phrase "life on life" but named by no letter, though Mark 3:14 makes being with him the first thing said about the Twelve. And *restoration after failure* is folded into Accountability, which is not the same thing: accountability drifts toward monitoring performance, while the Gospels put the restoring of a denier at the center of what it means to be re-commissioned (John 21:15–19). Also worth saying plainly: Matt 28:19's own participles include *baptizing*, which belongs to the church and to no small group.

**Rejoinder.** Nothing prevents a session from adding what the inventory lacks. If the argument for a model is that it codifies the definition, then testing it against the definition is the proper use of the argument, not an objection to it — and the test produces a better model.

**Author's rating: firm that this is the right way to argue for a model, and it is still not enough to make one binding.** The content is binding; a human summary of the content is not (A3). Two cautions must travel with this argument wherever it is made.

*Caution 1 — a codification can become a substitute for the thing codified.* The checklist gets completed while the substance is absent. This is not a hypothetical failure mode; it is the one Jesus named in men who tithed mint and dill and neglected justice, mercy, and faithfulness (Matt 23:23), and the one James names in hearers who are not doers (Jas 1:22–25). A group can run the curriculum faithfully for three years and produce no one who denies himself, and the framework will not register it.

*Caution 2 — the argument proves the content, not the container.* If TEAMS earns its place by matching the definition, then any other framework that matches the definition earns the same place. That cuts against binding TEAMS on anyone, and it cuts against dismissing a church that delivers the same content in a shape Perimeter would not recognize (Rom 14:4–5).

### E3. "Structured relational discipleship produces two-tier Christianity."

**Case for the danger.** Selection creates tiers by design. Partiality is sin (Jas 2:1–4; 1 Tim 5:21); the weakest members are given the greatest honor (1 Cor 12:22–25); Diotrephes liked to put himself first (3 John 9). And this is not hypothetical, though the historical case needs stating fairly. The Shepherding movement of the 1970s was a charismatic network built on covenantal submission over careers, marriages, and finances, outside any church court — structurally unlike a curriculum-based group under a session. Grant that difference first. What remains instructive is that it called itself the discipleship movement, began with spiritual maturity as its stated aim, and still produced documented domination and elitism, with its leaders later repenting publicly of "unhealthy submission resulting in perverse and unbiblical obedience to human leaders." The drift was gradual and the intentions were good.

**Counter — selectivity is not an embarrassment to be managed; it is the pattern.** Scripture is not neutral on this, and the concentration is deliberate at every level.

- *Jesus concentrated in concentric circles.* Crowds were taught (Matt 5:1–2); seventy-two were sent (Luke 10:1); twelve were appointed to be with him (Mark 3:14); within the Twelve, three were taken where the others were not — Jairus's house (Mark 5:37), the Transfiguration (Mark 9:2), Gethsemane (Mark 14:33); and one is repeatedly identified as the disciple whom Jesus loved (John 13:23; 19:26; 21:20). Four levels of concentration, in the ministry of the one man who had no sin to repent of in the arrangement.
- *Paul was selective by name.* He chose Timothy at Lystra on the strength of the brothers' report (Acts 16:1–3), called him "my beloved and faithful child in the Lord" (1 Cor 4:17), and said plainly, "I have no one like him" (Phil 2:20). That is a comparison, and Paul made it in writing.
- *So were others.* Barnabas took Saul when the church would not (Acts 9:27; 11:25–26), and later took Mark when Paul would not (Acts 15:37–39). Moses judged all Israel and trained Joshua (Exod 33:11). Elijah poured himself into Elisha (1 Kgs 19:19–21).
- *Creaturely limitation makes it unavoidable.* Jethro's counsel was given precisely because Moses could not do it alone (Exod 18:17–18). Finite men have finite hours. A church that refuses to concentrate anyone's attention has not avoided selection; it has made it accidental rather than prayerful.
- *And the concentration ran outward.* The Twelve were sent to crowds. The three became witnesses whose testimony the church still rests on (2 Pet 1:16–18). Timothy was left in Ephesus for a congregation's sake. Investment in a few was never terminal; it was instrumental (2 Tim 2:2).

**Rejoinder — the danger is real, but it is not located where the objection places it.** The texts against partiality do not forbid unequal investment of time; they forbid unequal *regard for persons*. James 2 is about seating the man with the gold ring above the poor man — judgment by worldly status. 1 Tim 5:21 is about prejudging a case. Neither addresses how a pastor allocates his week. So the sin is not concentration; it is valuing some souls above others.

But note *how* Scripture selects, because this is where models actually go wrong. Jesus chose fishermen, a tax collector, a zealot, and Judas — not the promising. "Not many were wise according to worldly standards… God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise" (1 Cor 1:26–29). Selection aimed at high potential inverts the pattern; selection aimed at those who will be with you and then go inverts nothing.

**Author's rating: firm that selectivity is biblical; the burden shifts to mechanisms.** Concentration needs no apology — it has better textual support than the objection does. What the objection legitimately demands is not that we stop selecting but that we answer five questions in advance: Who audits a group? Are we selecting for potential or for need? What is the exit ramp? What happens to a member who joins nothing — is he visited, or is he a category? And what happens when a leader is simply out of his depth — bad counsel, a mishandled confession, someone's marriage discussed by a man not equipped for it? That last is the likeliest harm in any real congregation, more likely than authoritarianism, and guardrails written against abuse do not cover it. Aspirational language is not an answer to any of the five.

### E4. "A model may function as a leadership pipeline."

**Case.** Some capable people will emerge from any serious discipleship effort, and the church needs officers, teachers, and leaders. Producing them is a benefit, not a defect.

**Counter.** It is a benefit, not a purpose, and models drift toward what they measure. Matthew's discipleship language runs toward "littleness," not leadership (Grams): humility is nowhere presented as a leadership qualification. Matthew calls followers prophets, righteous persons, disciples, wise men, and scribes (10:41–42; 23:34) — the "leaders" strand is one of five. Selection aimed at the promising rather than the weak inverts 1 Thess 5:14 and 1 Cor 12:22–25.

**Rejoinder.** Multiplication and littleness are not opposed; the Twelve were sent *and* told the greatest is the servant (Matt 20:26).

**Author's rating: firm as a caution, not as a claim either side needs to make.** Nobody has to argue the Gospels are a leadership manual for every-member discipleship to hold. But a model that starts sorting for potential has drifted, and this is where to catch it.

*Column: Prudence · Who does it: Officers · Use: Reference*

## F. Evidence claims

### F1. "Small groups produce mature disciples — the data shows it."

**Case.** Lifeway's 2026 analysis: discipleship scores of 74.1 for those in a group five or more times a month against 60.4 for those in none, with frequent group members far likelier to have been discipled one-on-one and to have discipled others.

**Counter.** Correlational and self-selected. People already growing join groups and stay. Causation is unestablished; outcomes are self-reported.

**Author's rating: open on causation.** Honest use: "consistent with," never "evidence for."

*Column: Evidence and history · What it is: Modeled, equipped, sent · Use: Do not use*

### F2. "Life-on-life at Perimeter works."

**Case.** Fifty years of practice, and the founder's account that attendance-based small groups were not reproducing believers while his groups were.

**Counter.** No published study — no dissertation, no peer review, no controlled comparison. Confounded with a large, well-staffed, affluent suburban congregation.

**Author's rating: open, and the honest move is to stop citing results, for both views.** The same standard falls on the status quo: no one has measured whether a church's present arrangement produces mature, reproducing disciples either. Skepticism applied only to proposals is not neutrality; it favors the status quo by default.

*Column: Evidence and history · What it is: Modeled, equipped, sent · Use: Do not use*

**Also lands:** No outcome study exists for this model or for the alternative.

## G. The historical claim

### G1. "The Reformed tradition grew deep disciples through preaching, without programs."

**Case.** Those who make this argument read the history this way: Puritan, Dutch, and Scottish churches produced remarkable maturity through preaching, with no discipleship structures.

**Counter.** False as stated, and this is a historical question, so historical evidence decides it. Baxter judged the older Elizabethan puritan model that focused solely on the pulpit deficient, and replaced it with systematic visitation, catechizing, and discipline — fourteen families a week, aiming at all eight hundred in the parish annually. The General Assembly of the Church of Scotland approved a Directory for Family Worship on 24 August 1647, three days before the same Assembly adopted the Westminster Confession — not the Westminster divines, but a body that judged their work incomplete without directions for "secret and private worship, and mutual edification," and that charged ministers and ruling elders with seeing it observed. The catechism assumes hearers confer on the Word with others (WLC 160). Our own polity requires elders to visit the people in their homes (BCO 8-3).

**Rejoinder — and a real limit.** Baxter catechized house to house *himself, as the minister*. That is officer-led pastoral visitation, which is the means-centered position's own practice, not every-member discipleship. He settles the narrow claim decisively and supports the other view on the wider one. He answers "preaching alone." He does not support every-member ministry.

**Author's rating: firm against the historical claim as stated, and no further.**

*Column: Evidence and history · What it is: Word and sacrament, received · Who does it: Officers · Use: Support*

## Where this leaves the argument

**Rated firm:** relationship is binding, not optional; the Commission commands teaching people *to keep*, not merely teaching them; the claim that attendance is a sufficient plan cannot survive Heb 3:13; selective, concentrated investment is the biblical pattern and needs no apology; discipline and household instruction are the instruments we already have and should be tested first; discipleship is the shape of the Christian life and never a condition of salvation; attendance alone is not discipleship; being sent and reproducing belong to the definition; members disciple; no model is commanded; the tradition already contains house-to-house practice.

**Rated contested:** where mutual admonition ends and the teaching office begins; how much of Jesus' practice binds; the scope of Eph 4:12 and 2 Tim 2:2; why the epistles drop the word "disciple" entirely.

**Rated open:** every empirical claim on the table, from any side — including any claim that our present arrangement is working.

**What the two axes show.** Tagging every argument twice — once for *what* discipleship is, once for *who* does it — turns up something the single-axis version had been hiding. Most bear on *what*; about half bear on *who* not at all. On the *who* question the split is much closer, and that is the genuinely open one. Note also where our case there rests: on Titus 2, Col 3:16, Heb 3:13 and D4 — not on the Great Commission, which is nearly silent about agency.

**The strongest argument against my own direction** is the one in D1 and D2: no text draws the line between mutual admonition and the teaching office, and the two verses most often used to draw it are contested. The every-member case is secure on the duty and unsettled on the boundary — which is exactly the question a session has to answer.
